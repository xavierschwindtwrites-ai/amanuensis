# Codex review of Claude's 14 ideas

2026-11-09. Review only; no application code changes.

For “maximum pull,” I would make the next scene more tempting, make entering its voice easier, and give the author stronger evidence of the story they have made. Several of your ideas support that. The cooling momentum glow is the one I would remove outright: gradual loss still makes absence visible.

## Verdicts

“Keep” retains the core experience, with the research and implementation qualifications below. “Revise” means the current version needs a material change.

| # | Idea | Verdict | One-line reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Stop mid-sentence | **Revise** | Offer an author-chosen return fragment or next move; do not infer that an unfinished sentence should become a launch prompt, and replace the Zeigarnik claim. |
| 2 | Ten-second spoken starting ritual | **Revise** | Make recap an explicit optional recording mode, with normal Record still immediate; a launch question and special Space behavior can add friction. |
| 3 | The book grows on the shelf | **Revise** | Keep tangible book volume, but replace word-threshold gilt rewards with a neutral display that does not make cutting words feel like losing finish or status. |
| 4 | Variable, rare, earned moments | **Revise** | Recognize meaningful author-chosen story milestones at pauses; remove the “strongest schedule” rationale and passage-length or chapter-size record chasing. |
| 5 | Goal-gradient finish line | **Revise** | Show truthful proximity at an author-requested pause or reveal, preserving concentration and the option to stop; “low risk” understates padding and overextension. |
| 6 | Overshoot bonus framing | **Revise** | Describe actual additional work once at a pause, with no live bonus accumulator or suggestion that stopping at the chosen goal leaves something unclaimed. |

## Research claims to correct or qualify

**1 and 10: Zeigarnik is the wrong certainty.** The 2025 meta-analysis distinguishes unfinished-task recall from task resumption: it found no overall recall advantage, but did find a resumption tendency. That does not establish that showing a last line will increase daily fiction writing. Curiosity, retrieval cues and reduced restart effort are reasonable hypotheses. Also, Hemingway's 1958 interview describes stopping while he still knows what happens next; that passage does not prescribe stopping mid-sentence. [Ghibellini & Meier, 2025][resumption]; [Hemingway interview][hemingway].

**2: Context-dependent habit research does not validate a ten-second spoken recap.** Wood and Neal's cue account is a reasonable starting point, but the specific ritual, duration and asserted removal of avoidance need testing here. A new ritual can itself become friction. Context repetition evidence supports consistency without implying that a missed occasion erases learning. [Lally et al., 2010][habits].

**3: Endowed progress and the IKEA effect are different claims.** Endowed progress concerns perceived advancement toward an outstanding goal, often supplied artificially. The IKEA experiments concern increased valuation of self-made products, with successful completion an important condition. Neither shows that richer cover ornament makes someone write more. Tangible progress and attachment to one's work are plausible interpretations; “risk: low” misses quantity fixation and loss during revision. [Nunes & Drèze, 2006][endowed]; [Norton, Mochon & Ariely, 2012][ikea].

**4: Rare feedback is not automatically a variable-ratio schedule, and “strongest” is too broad.** A variable-ratio schedule specifies reinforcement after varying numbers of responses. An occasional surprising fact does not establish that contingency or show that the fact functions as a reinforcer. Laboratory response rates and resistance to extinction do not identify the best design for sustained creative work. Say “occasional task-relevant feedback may be pleasurable,” then test that treatment. [Ferster & Skinner, 1957][schedules].

**4: Fresh starts are not a general synonym for milestones.** Dai, Milkman and Riis studied temporal landmarks that separate time into periods, such as a new week or birthday. Passing a novel's length or beating a passage record is not the manipulation they studied. An author-declared new phase of the book might be analogous; the analogy still needs qualification. [Dai, Milkman & Riis, 2014][fresh].

**5: Goal-gradient is credible; this exact treatment is not established.** Kivetz and colleagues support goal proximity effects in consumer tasks. A ten-percent threshold, warming ring, and hiding the initial gap are design choices, not findings established by that paper. A chosen goal can still induce rushing or padding. [Kivetz, Urminsky & Zheng, 2006][gradient].

**6: “Gain framing keeps going” needs evidence.** Your entry supplies no study that establishes this overshoot treatment. A real extra 340 words is factual feedback; calling them “bonus” and displaying an accumulating surplus can create a second goal. Treat increased persistence as a hypothesis, and assess whether the author still stops comfortably.

**7: The evidence supports goal attainment, not automatic habit formation.** The 2006 meta-analysis reported a medium-to-large average effect for implementation intentions. A 2024 synthesis of 642 tests reports effects varying across outcomes, so the older average should not become a promise for this author. Tie the plan to a cue outside the app: a reminder inside the app occurs after the opening behavior. [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006][implementation]; [2024 meta-analysis][implementation-update].

**8: Fogg's model does not prove that ease always beats motivation.** His formulation requires motivation, ability and a prompt to converge. It is a useful design model, not a universal ranking of interventions or evidence that fewer steps necessarily cause more writing sessions. “Under two seconds” is a performance hypothesis until measured on this app and Mac. [Fogg Behavior Model][fogg].

**9: Meaningful progress has evidence; playback-induced next scenes do not yet.** The progress principle comes from research on everyday meaningful work. It does not show that hearing one's recording causes the next scene to emerge or that a playback feature increases output. Immediate enjoyment is a reasonable additional mechanism; ask the author whether listening helps them want to write. [Amabile & Kramer][progress]; [Woolley & Fishbach, 2017][enjoyment].

**11: The streak concern is reasonable, but the citation and proposed solution do not match it.** Polivy and Herman's work concerns dietary restraint and bingeing. The abstinence-violation model is associated with Marlatt and Gordon; neither directly validates a decaying writing glow. Cite the direct broken-streak research instead. It does not establish that gradual decay removes the effect. More fundamentally, attendance is not automaticity: calling the glow “habit strength” claims knowledge the app lacks. [Polivy & Herman, 1985][diet]; [abstinence-violation study][abstinence]; [Silverman & Barasch, 2023][streaks].

**12: No evidence here supports detecting a stall at eight seconds.** Flow research makes absorption and task conditions relevant, but it does not validate this pause threshold or show that a faint prompt preserves flow. The author could be forming a difficult sentence. Use their explicit request for a cue. [Engeser & Rheinberg, 2008][flow].

**13: Peak-end is a rationale for testing memory of an ending, not a guarantee of return.** Classic research includes remembered aversive experiences and choices about repeating them. It does not establish that a 1.5-second title treatment makes a novelist return, or that the chapter boundary is the end of the relevant experience. The author may be recording straight into the next chapter. [Kahneman et al., 1993][peak-end].

**14: Separate identity research from the popular slogan.** James Clear offers practical synthesis; Oyserman's research does not reduce to “behavior follows self-concept.” Identity salience and context matter, including how people interpret difficulty. A cumulative word total is not evidence of identity or quality, and this exact header remains an untested cue. [Oyserman & Destin, 2010][identity].

## Codebase details that change scope

- **1:** Put the return fragment outside `#text`. `serialize()` clones that element, so grey ghost text inside it risks leaking into saved HTML. Ending a transcript passage does not reliably identify an unfinished grammatical sentence.
- **2 and 8:** `⌘J` currently opens a typed note editor. Optional “note to self” routing exists, but a dedicated hold-to-recap recorder needs explicit routing and input states. `boot()` already opens the last book and chapter, and `⌘D` already starts Record; the proposed gain is a shortcut choice, not a new re-entry system. “Armed” should not acquire microphone audio before the author's action.
- **4:** `sprintStats` and `dayStats` do not expose “longest unbroken passage this month.” That needs passage-level derivation and a definition of “unbroken”; transcript segmentation also depends on pause settings and provider behavior. The current `#today` hides during activity and on rest days. Keep those rules.
- **5 and 6:** There is no sprint progress ring. The current goal is daily net manuscript growth, not a session or chapter goal. Sprint overtime measures elapsed time beyond planned duration; it does not establish words beyond a goal. A ring or new goal scopes make this larger than a copy change.
- **9:** `play(r)` plays a time range from a recording, not an assembled manuscript rendition. Revised, deleted, typed and reordered text can diverge from audio; a spoken “new chapter” can also split a segment. A queue of retained original passages is plausible. Call it “Listen to today's dictation”; exact current-manuscript audio would be a separate, substantially larger problem.
- **10:** `lib:list` currently adds cover metadata, not chapter excerpts. A cached last-line field maintained during ordinary saves would avoid reading every manuscript just to show the shelf. In the current DOM, the next-time note is `#breadcrumb`; `#crumbbar` holds book/chapter navigation.
- **13:** `newChapter({dictating:true})` can run while recording continues. A completion ceremony needs an explicit completed state or author action, not an assumption based on chapter creation. Respect reduced motion and keep decoration outside serialized content.
- **14:** The ledger starts when Mastery is enabled, and deletion removes it. Deduplicate live dictation and label its start date; do not sum daily net growth and call it spoken words or imply coverage while tracking was off.

No version of these proposals needs changes to `lib/format.js` or the word guard. All additional state and processing can stay local, with no telemetry.

## Three of my ideas I would make bolder for maximum pull

These are deliberate, author-selected treatments. They can become more vivid without making rest costly or making rewards scarce. The effects below are design bets, not established increases in writing volume.

### My #2: Turn the waiting question into a next-scene invitation

Give the author a compact scene card: **the encounter they want to write, the question they want to discover, and the first thing they can say**. Example: “Leif corners Sable at the bridge. What would make her hand over the sword? Start with his accusation.” The author writes those lines; the app supplies no plot or prose.

Let them choose this card as their idle/return surface for that book, with its existing cover and a nearby Record action. It remains static when they return, then clears as soon as they begin. It need not add an extra launch step or wait for a scheduled session. An explicitly chosen fragment can sit beside it as context, outside the manuscript editor.

**Why bolder:** The author can anticipate a particular dramatic encounter instead of a generic writing session. Curiosity has a concrete object, and a first move reduces the effort of acting on it. This combines my #2 with #1; it does not rely on interrupting a sentence. [Loewenstein, 1994][curiosity]; [Gollwitzer & Sheeran, 2006][implementation].

**Fit and effort:** `renderer/app.js`, `lib/habits.js`, `renderer/index.html`, `renderer/styles.css`, book metadata; **M**. One visible scene card, no unresolved-question count, expiry or automatic resurfacing during recording.

### My #8: Make re-entry a short passage from the author's own voice

On request, “Take me back in” gives the author roughly 20–40 seconds from the chosen scene's saved recording, alongside the relevant read-only text and their return question. At its end, the view gives the caret back at the saved position with Record within reach. The author can skip playback immediately; the microphone stays off until they act.

**Why bolder:** This creates a stronger sensory return to the scene and its voices than a word total or generic encouragement. The duration is a starting design choice. Context reinstatement and enjoyment are plausible mechanisms; the author should judge whether hearing the recording creates a desire to continue. Label the audio as original dictation when edits have changed the page. [Smith & Vela, 2001][context]; [Woolley & Fishbach, 2017][enjoyment].

**Fit and effort:** Extend `renderer/app.js`'s `play`, passage selection and re-entry; presentation in `renderer/index.html`/`renderer/styles.css`; **M** for a source passage, more work for a cross-recording queue. No launch autoplay, audio reward unlocks or generated narration.

### My #14: Give completed story work a private visual record

Promote an optional receipt such as “Sable finally tells the truth” into a small scene card in an author-opened book journal. They can pair it with a favorite sentence and, where available, a short original-voice excerpt. Their book's cover typography supplies the visual identity. Show a small set of recent or pinned cards, with direct return to the relevant chapter.

The author can make a card for discovering an ending, cutting a wrong turn, or resolving a character problem. The app can prepare chapter references and existing counts; it must leave the meaning and wording to the author. No daily quota, completion badge, empty date slots or card collection target.

**Why bolder:** The author can encounter scenes they made and anticipate making the next one. This gives narrative progress more presence than a transient acknowledgement. It combines my #14 with #13's favorite lines and your playback idea. [Amabile & Kramer][progress]; [Woolley & Fishbach, 2017][enjoyment].

**Fit and effort:** `renderer/app.js` session and chapter hooks, an author-opened journal surface, `renderer/mastery.js` review entry, `renderer/covers.js` visual vocabulary, metadata outside the ledger; **M–L**. Make saving a receipt optional and available even when revision reduces word count.

I would test the **scene invitation first**, then add the **voice return**, then the **story journal** if the author likes saving receipts. The first two act directly on starting the next scene; the third makes the accumulated work more rewarding to revisit. The author can judge whether each makes them want to write more while retaining a comfortable stopping point.
